Research

I study how people live with subscriptions, and how they respond to emerging technologies.

My research examines how consumers experience, evaluate, and respond to digital and technology-enabled market offerings. Two streams run through my work: the recurring commitments of the subscription economy, and consumers’ reactions to emerging technologies, which are connected by a shared interest in convenience, control, and well-being in modern markets.

Dissertation

The Weight of Convenience

Subscription Fatigue, Coping, and Sources of Stress in Recurring Consumption

Subscriptions are designed to make life easier, one service at a time. But in reality, people often manage many subscriptions at once. Streaming, software, food delivery, fitness, news, and other digital services can quickly add up. My dissertation looks at subscription fatigue, or the mental, emotional, and financial burden that can come from managing many recurring commitments at the same time.

Rather than focusing mainly on why companies use subscriptions or how they keep customers, I look at the consumer side of the experience. I study how people feel when subscriptions start to become overwhelming, what they do to manage that burden, and which features of subscription models make the experience easier or more stressful.

Stream I — Subscription Economy · Working paper

Losing Control Behind the Wheel: How Subscription Models Shape Consumer Ownership and Fairness Perceptions

Samira Yaabdollahi & Vahid Yousefi

What happens when companies embed features in physical products but require consumers to pay a subscription to use them? This project examines how consumers respond to subscription-based access to product features. Reddit discussions and topic modeling identify key consumer concerns, while a scenario-based experiment tests how subscription access, compared with upfront payment, can reduce purchase intentions through perceived price unfairness and weakened psychological ownership.

Read the abstract

As firms increasingly shift from product-oriented to service-oriented strategies, many are embedding add-on features into physical products while restricting access through subscription-based activation. Study 1 uses web-scraped Reddit discussions and topic modeling to explore consumers’ reactions to subscription-based locked features, identifying perceived price unfairness and reduced psychological ownership as two central areas of concern. Study 2 uses a scenario-based experiment to examine how subscription-based access, compared with upfront payment, influences purchase intentions through these two mechanisms — and whether feature innovativeness and feature type (safety versus convenience) shape consumers’ responses. The research shows how firms’ continued control over features embedded in legally owned products can transform a pricing decision into broader judgments about fairness, control, and ownership.

Both studies collected · manuscript in development · target:  Journal of the Association for Consumer Research

03
Stream II — Emerging Technologies · Working paper

Thinking about the Future, Feeling the Present: How Future-Oriented Expectations Shape Robot Technology Acceptance

Alec Slepchuk, George Milne & Samira Yaabdollahi

Some consumers love robots, some fear them, and many feel both. This project develops the Consumer Robot Typology, a two-dimensional segmentation built on when people believe robots may surpass human capabilities and whether that future would be good or bad for society. Across three studies pairing biometric and self-reported data, future-oriented beliefs shape present emotions, which in turn drive perceived usefulness and ease of use.

Read the abstract

Consumers have limited experience with emerging technologies, making expectations about these technologies critical to acceptance. Although research has examined cognition and emotion in technology adoption, the role of future-oriented expectations remains unclear. We address this gap by introducing future-oriented expectations as an upstream psychological mechanism underlying technology acceptance. We operationalize these expectations with the Consumer Robot Typology (CRT), a parsimonious psychographic framework that segments consumers according to the anticipated timing and societal consequences of superhuman robots. Drawing upon Appraisal Theory, Construal Level Theory, and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), we propose that expectations shape consumers' emotional responses, which subsequently influence technology acceptance. Across three complementary studies employing biometric and self-reported measures, we find that anticipated timing and societal consequences predict current emotions, which in turn predict robot technology acceptance. Consumers thus evaluate emerging technologies not only by what they are today, but by the futures they invite consumers to imagine.

Under review at:  Journal of Business Research

Stream II — Emerging Technologies · In progress

Different Ways to Trust AI: How Mechanical and Learning AI Shape Consumer Responses

Samira Yaabdollahi & George Milne

Consumers don’t simply trust or distrust “AI”; different kinds of intelligence may invite different kinds of trust. This project explores how the nature of an AI system shapes the kind of trust consumers place in it and how that trust influences their responses to the technology.

Collaboration — Sales Force & Frontline Employees · In progress

A Meta-Analytical Assessment of the Push–Pull–Mooring Theory

Vahid Yousefi, Samira Yaabdollahi & Hossein Hashemi

Why do salespeople and frontline employees leave, and what makes them stay? This meta-analysis synthesizes the empirical record on the push of dissatisfaction, the pull of outside alternatives, and the mooring forces that anchor people in place, clarifying which forces matter most for turnover and retention across contexts.
