Research

I study how people live with subscriptions, and how they learn to trust intelligent machines.

My research examines how consumers experience, evaluate, and respond to digital and technology-enabled market offerings. Two streams run through my work — the recurring commitments of the subscription economy, and consumers’ reactions to emerging technologies — bound by a shared question about convenience, control, and well-being in modern markets.

Stream I — Subscription Economy Stream II — Emerging Technologies
01
Dissertation · Job-Market Paper

The Weight of Convenience

Subscription Fatigue, Coping, and Sources of Stress in Recurring Consumption

Subscriptions promise ease one service at a time — but consumers do not live one service at a time. Across streaming, software, delivery, fitness, news, and digital services, this dissertation develops subscription fatigue as a portfolio-level experience: the cumulative cognitive, emotional, and financial weight of managing many recurring commitments at once.

Where prior research has viewed subscriptions through firm-centered lenses of access, retention, and monetization, this project asks what recurring-payment systems do to the people inside them: how fatigue is experienced, the coping strategies consumers deploy — restructuring portfolios, tracking payments, sharing access, rationalizing — and which subscription structures create or intensify the strain.

02
Stream I — Subscription Economy · Working paper

Losing Control Behind the Wheel: How Subscription Models Shape Consumer Ownership and Fairness Perceptions

Samira Yaabdollahi & Vahid Yousefi

When carmakers lock features into vehicles and sell them back by subscription, what happens to ownership? Reddit discussions and topic modeling surface consumers’ reactions; a scenario-based experiment then tests how subscription access — versus upfront payment — dampens purchase intentions through perceived price unfairness and weakened psychological ownership, and when feature innovativeness or safety relevance softens the response.

Read the abstract

As firms increasingly shift from product-oriented to service-oriented strategies, many are embedding add-on features into physical products while restricting access through subscription-based activation. Study 1 uses web-scraped Reddit discussions and topic modeling to explore consumers’ reactions to subscription-based locked features, identifying perceived price unfairness and reduced psychological ownership as two central areas of concern. Study 2 uses a scenario-based experiment to examine how subscription-based access, compared with upfront payment, influences purchase intentions through these two mechanisms — and whether feature innovativeness and feature type (safety versus convenience) shape consumers’ responses. The research shows how firms’ continued control over features embedded in legally owned products can transform a pricing decision into broader judgments about fairness, control, and ownership.

Both studies collected · manuscript in development · target:  Journal of the Association for Consumer Research

03
Stream II — Emerging Technologies · Working paper

Thinking about the Future, Feeling the Present: The Consumer Robot Typology

Alec Slepchuk, George Milne & Samira Yaabdollahi

Some consumers love robots, some fear them — many feel both. This project develops the Consumer Robot Typology, a two-dimensional segmentation built on when people believe robots may surpass human capabilities and whether that future would be good or bad for society. Across three studies pairing biometric and self-reported data, future-oriented beliefs shape present emotions, which in turn drive perceived usefulness and ease of use — with positive emotions doing surprisingly heavy lifting.

Read the abstract

Consumers have varied emotional reactions to robots; some love them, some hate them, and others are ambivalent. These emotions have important implications for robot technology acceptance, yet firms adopting these technologies cannot easily assess consumers’ feelings toward robots. We address this gap by developing a two-dimensional psychographic segmentation scheme based on consumers’ future-oriented cognitions about robots — the Consumer Robot Typology (CRT). Drawing on construal-level and emotion theories, we propose that CRT segments differ systematically in their current emotional responses and, in turn, their acceptance of robot technologies. Using biometric and self-reported data across three studies, we find that emotions mediate the relationships between the CRT, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use.

Manuscript in preparation · presented at Macromarketing 2026 · target:  Journal of Business Research

Stream II — Emerging Technologies · In progress

Different Ways to Trust AI: How Mechanical and Learning AI Shape Consumer Responses

Samira Yaabdollahi & George Milne

Consumers don’t simply trust or distrust “AI” — different kinds of intelligence invite different kinds of trust. An online experiment varying AI type and humanoid level in a home-assistant context suggests mechanical AI strengthens perceptions of competence and integrity, while learning AI aligns more closely with benevolence. Follow-up studies will trace how these trust pathways shape behavior and identify their boundary conditions.

One study collected · follow-up studies planned

Collaboration — Sales Force & Frontline Employees · In progress

A Meta-Analytical Assessment of the Push–Pull–Mooring Theory

Vahid Yousefi, Samira Yaabdollahi & Hossein Hashemi

Why do salespeople and frontline employees leave — and what makes them stay? This meta-analysis synthesizes the empirical record on the push of dissatisfaction, the pull of outside alternatives, and the mooring forces that anchor people in place, clarifying which forces matter most for turnover and retention across contexts.
